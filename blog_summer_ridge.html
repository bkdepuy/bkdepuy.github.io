The Summer of the Ridge

A story about a valley town, a baseball season, a parking lot conversation, and what happens when the people who are supposed to help don't show up — and someone else does.

Bradley K. DePuy  ·  Levy County, Florida, 2026
Chapter One — The Valley Chapter Two — The Parking Lot
Chapter One

The Valley

The summer I'm telling you about, the hills above the valley were still dairy country. You knew it by the smell when the wind came down off the north face — that green-and-manure smell that sounds unpleasant if you didn't grow up with it but was just the smell of the world working the way it was supposed to. Black and white Holsteins on the hillside pastures. The silver cylinders of the bulk tanks catching the morning light. Every farm had a name on the mailbox and you knew the name and the family both.

What you didn't know yet, what none of us knew, was that most of those farms would be gone inside of twenty years. The squeeze was already on — milk prices, operating costs, the banks getting impatient — but it hadn't announced itself. It was happening the way weather happens before you see the clouds, a pressure in the air, a change in how the light falls. My grandfather could feel it, I think. He didn't say so. You didn't speak the bad thing until you had to.

The volunteer fire department had been busy that spring with barn fires. Three in six weeks, all of them hay. You put up bales with any moisture in them and the fermentation builds heat you can't see or smell until the whole interior of the stack is smoldering, and by then you have maybe minutes before the barn goes. The dairy farmers knew this and were careful and still it happened because careful isn't the same as infallible and a barn full of dry hay in August is its own kind of argument.

After the second fire the chief started holding informal talks at the long table on Thursday mornings about hay moisture and stack spacing and the signs to watch for. Farmers came in who weren't members. Women came whose husbands had been up on the hoses and wanted to know what to tell them. The table got crowded and someone dragged in folding chairs and Edna Marsh from the diner started sending over a second coffee urn without being asked.

That's what I mean about the fire hall. It wasn't a club. It was where the town thought out loud.

—

The town itself sat in a fold of the valley where two creeks met before joining the river south of the fairgrounds. Mill Street ran four blocks from the war memorial to the railroad siding that hadn't seen freight in a decade but whose tracks were still there, still crossed carefully by habit. The hardware store. The diner. The barbershop with the same magazines from the previous year. The fire hall at the corner with its two bay doors almost always open in summer, and inside, the truck — one truck, red, kept clean as a kitchen — and beyond the truck the long table where the men gathered on Thursday evenings and most Saturday mornings and whenever there was weather coming or news worth discussing.

Not everyone in town belonged to the department. You had to be able to do the work, and the work was not small. But everyone had someone who belonged, or had belonged, and the table on Thursday mornings was understood to be more or less public. My grandfather drank his coffee there most mornings of his adult life. He said it was the only place in town where you found out what was actually happening rather than what people wished was happening.

I asked him once what the difference was.

He thought about it the way he thought about most things — without hurrying.

"Wishes travel faster," he said. "Facts have to walk."

—

The high school was the other pole of the town's social gravity. Not the building exactly — a brick rectangle that could have been anywhere — but what happened inside and around it. Friday nights in autumn the whole valley came out. Farmers who hadn't left their land all week. Women who worked double shifts at the cannery. Old men who had played on that same field forty years before and watched their sons play and now watched their grandsons with the same mixture of hope and private knowledge that things would not go as planned.

It wasn't really about football.

It was the one ritual that required nothing of you except to show up. No skills, no membership, no particular belief. You came and stood in the cold and drank bad coffee from a paper cup and you were of the place. That was enough.

My grandmother knew everyone there by name and history. She knew who was having trouble with a son and who had just paid off their mortgage and who was quietly drinking too much and whose marriage was holding by a thread. She didn't gossip with this knowledge. She used it the way a doctor uses a chart — to know how to be useful.

That was the texture of it. Everyone carrying a piece of the communal knowledge, everyone responsible for their corner of it. Information wasn't delivered to you. You assembled it yourself from what you saw and heard and touched and smelled, from a hundred conversations at the fire hall table and the football field fence and the hardware store counter and the church steps after service.

You knew things because you had been places and talked to people and worked alongside them and watched what they did when it was hard.

—

That year our high school baseball team was having the kind of season that happens maybe once in a generation — the kind where everything that should go wrong doesn't, and everything that might go right does, and the boys themselves seem almost surprised each time, like they can't quite believe they're the ones doing this.

This was not Little League. Not any affiliated league at all, really. Every town in the county had a team, and the teams played each other on fields that had been ballfields since before the players' fathers were born, with hand-painted scoreboards and wooden bleachers that flexed under you and a chain-link backstop that had been patched so many times the patches had patches. The umpires were local men who called it as they saw it and argued back when argued with, which meant you never quite knew what you were going to get behind the plate, which was itself part of the game.

By late June we were winning more than we were losing, which was already more than anyone had expected. By the Fourth of July we were in the championship.

—

The Fourth was always the center of the summer. The fairgrounds on the flat south of town — the same grounds used for the county fair in September, still smelling of September even in July, that particular mix of trampled grass and diesel from the generator trucks and something sweet from the food stands that you could never quite identify. The volunteer auxiliary ran the food. The fire department ran the games of chance, which were not quite chance. The selectmen gave brief speeches that nobody listened to but everyone appreciated for their brevity.

And at three o'clock, the baseball game.

I don't remember the name of the town we played. Harmon, maybe, or something like it — a town about our size from the next valley over, with a team that had beaten us twice in the last four years and whose pitcher, a left-hander whose name I've lost, had a curveball that had been talked about all season the way weather is talked about.

The bleachers filled early. The overflow went to lawn chairs along the first and third base lines, and when those filled up, to the cars parked along the outfield fence. By game time there were vehicles two deep out there, their hoods and rooftops occupied, and when something happened worth noting — a good play, a hit that found the gap, a throw from the outfield that caught a runner at the plate — you heard the horns before you heard the crowd, a sound that was somewhere between celebration and startlement, everyone honking at once so that the field was surrounded by this democratic noise, everyone contributing the same note, no one voice louder than another.

I have never heard anything quite like it since. It was what agreement sounds like when it's genuine — not manufactured, not organized, just the same feeling moving through people at the same time and finding the same expression.

My grandfather stood at the third base line with his arms folded and watched the game with the same attention he gave everything — complete, unhurried, missing nothing.

Burwell was there too, of course. Burwell was everywhere the town gathered. He had strong opinions about the team's strategy that he shared with anyone standing near him, and a running commentary on the umpire's calls that was not always charitable. But even Burwell, during the good moments — and there were good moments, our shortstop made a play in the fifth inning that people would describe for years — even Burwell just honked his horn. Same note as everyone else.

—

We won. I'll tell you that now rather than make you wait, because the game is not the point of the story exactly — or rather, the game is the frame that holds the point.

We won in the seventh inning on a double that scored two runs and then held on through a nerve-shredding eighth when their cleanup hitter hit a shot to deep left that our left fielder — a quiet boy named Geer who nobody had thought much about until that season — caught running full speed with his back to the plate.

The horns when that catch happened were something else entirely. Sustained, overlapping, some of them musical and some of them just noise, but all of them saying the same thing.

We are here. We are the same thing right now.

—

Now I have to tell you about Burwell.

He was not a bad man. That is important to say and I want you to hold onto it, because the story is easier if he is simply a villain and he wasn't. He genuinely loved the town. He worried about it the way you worry about something you feel responsible for protecting. There were things he said that were true — enough true things threaded through that you had to pay attention to find where they ended and the other things began.

He said the valley to the east was failing because of people who didn't share the town's values. He said the water troubles two counties over were caused by outsiders who didn't understand how things worked here. He said the young people who left and came back changed had been gotten to somehow, turned around, and couldn't be fully trusted anymore.

He said these things at the hardware store and the diner and sometimes, if the fire chief wasn't paying attention, at the long table on Thursday mornings.

Some people nodded. It was easier to nod. They were tired, most of them, tired in the way that people with real work are tired, and Burwell was offering to do their thinking for them on matters that felt large and distant, and that seemed like a reasonable trade.

My grandfather did not nod.

He didn't argue either, mostly. He'd learned that arguing with Burwell was like trying to bail a boat with a fork — you'd exhaust yourself and the water wouldn't care.

What my grandfather did was ask questions. Not combative questions. Genuine ones, or ones that sounded genuine.

"How do you know that, Ed?"

"Who told you that first?"

"Have you been over there yourself?"

Burwell usually had an answer. But the answers, when you listened carefully, were always another voice. Someone had told him. He had heard. There was talk. The source of the knowledge, if you followed it back through the chain of tellings, had a way of dissolving into vapor before you reached anything solid.

My grandfather would nod at this — not Burwell's nod, which was agreement, but his own nod, which was filing something away — and drink his coffee and say nothing more.

Walking home once I asked him why he didn't just say so. Say that Burwell didn't know what he was talking about.

"Because he's not lying," my grandfather said. "He believes it. And you can't argue a man out of something he believes if he doesn't trust your evidence any more than his own."

He watched a crow cross the road ahead of us.

"You can only give him a reason to go look for himself. And you can't even give him that if he's stopped talking to you."

"So what do you do?"

"You stay at the table," he said. "You ask the questions. You let him hear himself answer. And sometimes — not always, but sometimes — a man will follow his own answer somewhere it surprises him."

—

That evening, after the fairgrounds emptied and the auxiliary had packed up the food stands and the generator trucks had gone dark, my grandfather and I walked the long way home past the fire hall.

The bay doors were open. A few of the men were inside going over the truck — there had been a grass fire south of town earlier that the department had knocked down in an hour, but the equipment had to be checked and restowed regardless. The work of readiness is mostly invisible and mostly never stops.

Burwell was holding court on the sidewalk outside. He had been moved by the game, you could see that — his color was up and he was warm in a way that was genuine rather than his usual performed authority. He was talking about the team and about the town and about how things like this were what made the valley special, and how there were forces that wanted to take it away, people from outside who didn't understand what this was and didn't care to learn.

A few men nodded. They were tired and happy and it was easy to nod.

My grandfather stopped. He listened to Burwell for a moment, his arms still folded the same way they'd been folded at the third base line.

Then he said, pleasantly, "What forces, Ed?"

Burwell explained. There were people to the east buying up land. There were regulations coming down from the state designed — and he used that word, designed, as if someone had sat in a room and planned it — to strangle the small farmer. There were outside interests that wanted this valley to be something other than what it was.

"Where'd you hear that?" my grandfather asked.

Burwell gestured vaguely. There had been something on the radio. Someone at the hardware store had mentioned it. His cousin in the next county had written him a letter.

"Have you talked to any of the dairy farmers about it?" my grandfather asked. "About what they're actually seeing?"

Burwell hadn't, specifically. He knew what they were going through, of course. Everyone did.

"Harold Esten was at the table Tuesday," my grandfather said. "He'd tell you the real problem is what they're getting per hundredweight versus what it costs to run the operation. That's not outside interests. That's math."

He paused. "Though I suppose the math came from somewhere."

He said it without sarcasm. As if he was genuinely thinking it through, which perhaps he was.

Burwell started to say something and then didn't. One of the other men — a younger man, maybe thirty, who had been on the hoses for two of the barn fires that spring — was watching my grandfather with an expression I didn't have a name for at fourteen but would come to recognize later. It was the expression of a person hearing something that doesn't match what they've been carrying, and feeling the mismatch before they've decided what to do about it.

My grandfather wished everyone a good evening and we walked on.

—

The dairy farms did decline. Most of them were gone by the time I had children of my own. Whether anything could have been done about it — whether the math could have been different, whether the forces Burwell described were real or were stories about real things or were real things described so badly they became useless as information — I honestly don't know. These questions are harder than I understood at fourteen.

What I know is that Carver's Mill faced whatever it faced better when it thought with its own mind. When the fire chief sat at that long table on Thursday mornings and the farmers came in and they worked through the problem of wet hay together — that was the town at its best. Nobody was told what to think. Everybody brought what they knew from their own barn, their own pasture, their own experience. The answer that came out of it was made of real things.

Burwell's answers were made of other people's fears, passed along until nobody could find the beginning of them.

The difference matters. It mattered then. It matters now more than it did then.

My grandfather's question — the one he returned to, patient as the ridge, whenever Burwell held forth — was always the same question, just differently dressed.

"How do you know that?"

Not as an accusation. As a genuine inquiry. As the thing a person asks when they actually want to know.

It is still the most important question I know.

How do you know.

Not who told you. Not what you heard. Not what everyone around you agrees on.

Go find out.

Chapter Two

The Parking Lot

The town meeting was held in the fire hall because the school gymnasium had a broken boiler that the district couldn't afford to fix that winter, and because the fire hall was where serious things happened in Carver's Mill anyway. Folding chairs were set up facing a table where three men sat with papers in front of them — the county commissioner, a man from the agricultural extension office, and a state representative named Dodd who had driven up from the capital that afternoon and would drive back that night.

Representative Dodd had good hair and a firm handshake and he knew how to stand in a room full of farmers the way a man knows how to stand when he has practiced it. He said folks and our way of life and the backbone of this country and he was not lying exactly when he said these things — he had grown up in a town not entirely unlike this one, three counties over, and he retained a genuine if increasingly theoretical affection for what it represented.

The room was full. I had not seen the fire hall that full since the barn fires, when the chief had brought everyone together over the hay. But this was different. The barn fires had been a practical problem with practical solutions and everyone in the room had had something to contribute from their own experience. This was something else. This was people who had been called together to be told something, and they knew it, and they had come anyway because there was nowhere else to go.

Ray's father sat three chairs to my left. I knew him the way you know everyone in a town that size — his face, his truck, the name on the mailbox at the end of the long drive up to the farm. Harold Esten sat behind him. The Morse brothers near the door. Men whose families had been in that valley longer than the road had.

Dodd spoke first and at some length. There were changes coming in the federal dairy support program. The structure was being revised. There were transition resources available, programs designed to help farms adapt, the extension office would be the point of contact for anyone who wanted to discuss their specific situation. The state was committed to rural communities. Had always been committed. Would remain committed.

He said all of this the way you say something you have said before, to rooms like this one, and will say again.

When he finished Ray's father raised his hand.

He was not an aggressive man. He was a man who got up before dawn and worked until dark and kept his accounts in a green ledger his own father had used and knew to the penny what the operation cost and what it returned. He asked his question the way he did everything — directly, without performance.

What would the price support be next year. Specifically. Per hundredweight.

Dodd looked at the man from the extension office. The man from the extension office looked at his papers.

There were factors, Dodd said. There were processes underway at the federal level. There were variables that made it difficult to give a specific figure at this time. He would look into it and get back to them.

Ray's father nodded.

It was the nod of a man who had his answer. Not the answer to the question he had asked. The answer to the question underneath it, the one he hadn't needed to ask out loud.

I was fifteen that night. Ray was sitting next to his father and he was fifteen too, and I watched his face while his father nodded and I saw something happen there that I didn't have words for then. I have words for it now. It was the moment a person decides, without deciding, that the official version of things has nothing left to offer him. Not in anger. In a quiet that is somehow worse than anger because it doesn't leave a door open.

—

Burwell was in the parking lot after.

Of course he was. Burwell had a sense for these moments the way certain animals sense weather — something in the pressure changed and he was already positioned when it broke.

He wasn't holding forth. That was the thing I remember most clearly. He was just standing by his truck in the cold with two or three men around him and he was talking quietly, almost gently, the way you talk to someone after a loss. Which is what it was.

Ray and his father came out and Burwell nodded at them and said something I couldn't hear and Ray's father stopped walking.

I don't know everything that was said. I was at the edge of it, close enough to see but not clearly enough to hear every word. What I could hear was the shape of it — the way Burwell's voice stayed low and certain, the way he said they with a particular weight, the way he named something and the naming seemed to give Ray's father somewhere to put what he was carrying.

Ray stood beside his father and listened. He was fifteen and his father's farm was in the process of being taken from them by forces that had no face and no name and no address you could drive to and no meeting you could attend and demand an answer from — and then here was Burwell in the parking lot with a face and a name and an address for it, and the face looked like an enemy worth having, which is to say it looked like something you could actually fight.

I saw Ray's shoulders change. That's the only way I can describe it. Something that had been held came down slightly, the way a man's shoulders come down when he has been given permission to stop carrying something alone.

He was wrong. What Burwell was giving him was not relief but a different kind of weight, one that would sit on those shoulders for decades and get heavier rather than lighter. But in that parking lot, in the cold, after the nod his father had given in the fire hall, it felt like solid ground.

—

I didn't talk to Ray that night. We were fifteen and not particularly close, the way boys the same age in a small town are not particularly close — you know each other completely in some ways and barely at all in others.

What I did was walk home with my grandfather, who had been at the meeting, who had watched Dodd the way he watched Burwell — not with hostility, but with the particular attention of a man calibrating how much weight to give what he was hearing.

"He didn't know," I said. Meaning Dodd. Meaning the price per hundredweight.

"He knew," my grandfather said.

I thought about that. "Then why didn't he say?"

My grandfather walked a few steps without answering. The cold was the kind that makes your breath visible and the stars very clear and the distance between where you are and everything else very apparent.

"Because knowing and saying are different problems," he said finally. "Knowing doesn't cost him anything. Saying costs him the next election."

"That's—"

"Yes," he said. Before I finished.

We walked a while longer.

"So what does Ray's father do?"

"He does what men do when the people who are supposed to help them don't," my grandfather said. "He finds someone who at least tells him the truth about the fact that he's been failed. Even if the rest of it isn't true."

He said it without bitterness. As a plain description of how things work. Which made it worse somehow than bitterness would have.

"Is there anything—"

"Not tonight," he said. "Not in that parking lot."

—

I saw Ray grow into it over the years the way you see a tree grow into a fence — slowly, and then completely, until you can't tell anymore where the tree ends and the wire begins.

By the time we were in our twenties the farm was gone and Ray was working at the county garage and Burwell's framework had become the water Ray swam in — not Burwell's voice specifically anymore but the thing Burwell's voice had opened up, which was now arriving from everywhere, the radio, the television, the men at the diner, each one confirming what the others said, each confirmation making the next one easier to receive.

The thing I noticed — and this took me years to see clearly — was that the framework had a particular relationship to evidence. It wasn't that it ignored evidence. It processed evidence. But it processed everything in one direction, the way a one-way valve works. Anything that confirmed what Ray already believed passed through immediately and became fact. Anything that complicated or contradicted it got explained — it was biased, it was manufactured, it came from people with an agenda, you couldn't trust the source.

Over time this meant the framework became perfectly sealed. Not because Ray was stupid — he wasn't, he could read a parts diagram and fix an engine by sound and knew more about the weather and the land than most people with advanced degrees in such things. But the sealing was complete. No new information in.

I thought sometimes about what my grandfather had said — you can't argue a man out of something he believes if he doesn't trust your evidence any more than his own. I had thought he meant it as a tactical observation, a reason for patience. I understood now he meant it as a description of something structural. The distrust of evidence wasn't a side effect. It was the foundation. Once you pulled that stone the whole thing came down, so the stone got mortared in very carefully and very early.

—

There was a rally on television one night — I was at the diner, the set was on above the counter — and a man on a stage was saying the things Ray said, in Ray's cadences, with Ray's certainties. The crowd behind him was full of Ray's face repeated, the same set to the jaw, the same recognition in the eyes of a person hearing their own experience finally named from a platform large enough to mean something.

The man on the stage had never been to a town like Carver's Mill except to stand on a stage. He lived in buildings with his name on them in cities he claimed to despise and his understanding of the valley and the farms and the price per hundredweight was, I would wager, roughly equivalent to Dodd's — theoretical, instrumental, useful for certain purposes.

But he had learned — or someone around him had learned — that the true grievance was the door. That if you stood in the doorway of the true grievance and named it loudly enough the people who lived inside it would follow you anywhere.

Ray was watching from somewhere. I knew that without knowing it. Ray was watching and hearing his father's life named from that stage, the loss and the abandonment and the gap between what was promised and what arrived — all of it true, all of it real, all of it being used.

I thought about a horse I'd read about in school. A horse who worked until he couldn't and believed until the very end, until he was loaded onto the truck. The specific tragedy of that horse wasn't that he had been deceived by villains. It was that his best qualities — his strength, his loyalty, his willingness to work harder, always harder — were the exact instruments of his undoing. The thing that made him admirable made him useful to people who were not admirable. And he never knew.

I didn't say any of this to the man at the counter next to me. He was watching the same screen and nodding slightly, the way Ray nodded, and there was nothing I could say in that diner in that moment that would have been worth the saying.

I thought about my grandfather.

You stay at the table. You ask the questions.

The table had gotten harder to sit at. The questions landed differently now. But I didn't know what else to do, and so I ordered more coffee and I stayed.

—

Years later I ran into Ray at the fire hall. There was a retirement dinner for the chief — a different chief by then, the third since the one I'd known as a boy — and most of the town came out for it because that is what the town still did, when something asked it to.

Ray looked the way men look when the years have confirmed rather than softened what they believed — settled, certain, the framework worn smooth by long use. He shook my hand and we talked about the chief and about the town and about who had left and who had stayed and who had died.

Then he said something that stopped me.

He said the valley had been destroyed. That word exactly — destroyed. And what he said next was almost word for word true — the farms were gone, the young people had left, the hardware store had closed, the diner was the only thing left on Mill Street that you could still recognize. The valley he had grown up in no longer existed in any functional sense. All of that was accurate.

And then in the same breath, without pausing, he told me who had done it and why, and the telling had the texture of something recited — not because Ray didn't believe it, he believed it completely, but because he had said it so many times that it had worn into a groove and ran there automatically, the true part and the constructed part fused into a single motion, inseparable.

I stood there holding the true grievance and the false explanation in the same sentence and I didn't know what to do with them. Because if I touched the false part Ray would hear it as a defense of the true part. That's the trap. The weld is the point. The anger at the loss and the explanation for the loss are joined so that you cannot question one without appearing to dismiss the other, and Ray had enough real losses that dismissing them was the one thing I was not willing to do.

So I said: "The farms going — that was real. Your father's farm. That was real."

Ray looked at me. Something shifted slightly in his face, the way it shifts when a person is heard rather than argued with, which doesn't happen to Ray as often as it should.

"Yeah," he said. "It was."

We stood there a moment in that.

I didn't ask the next question. My grandfather would have. My grandfather would have found the question that fit inside that moment and asked it quietly and let Ray follow his own answer somewhere. I wasn't my grandfather. I didn't have his patience or his precision and the evening was not the right place and Ray was not in the right place and maybe I wasn't either.

But I stayed at the table. We ate the dinner and I listened to Ray and I asked the occasional question and I did not argue and I did not agree and somewhere in that space — I don't know exactly where — Ray said something that surprised him. You could see it surprise him. A thought that came out of his own mouth that he hadn't quite assembled before and that didn't fit neatly inside the framework.

He didn't follow it. He let it go and moved on and the framework closed back around it. But it had been there. A quarter-beat pause in the certainty, the kind my grandfather used to watch for.

Not a victory. I know that. A quarter-beat pause is not a revolution.

But my grandfather said it was where everything started.
